Playground · Signals / Media / Hardware

Shuffletube

A receiver and mixer for signals that are already there.

Watch them. Control them. Bank time. Save them. Combine them. Let them collide. Shuffletube begins with free over-the-air television, but the experiment is broader: what happens when broadcast and open signals become ingredients instead of services you merely consume?

The broadcaster controls what is transmitted. Shuffletube explores how much control can return to you once the signal arrives.

The model

Signals become ingredients.

Television is the first source, not the boundary. OTA broadcasts, radio, shortwave, recordings and open network streams can become material for watching, listening, exploring and making.

AIR — OTA television · FM / AM · shortwave / SDR
NET — open radio · public streams · live data
ARCHIVE — recordings · podcasts · captured media

A small primitive

Boost

Start watching normally. Shuffletube quietly banks a few minutes of the live signal. Once enough time is available, spend it by skipping forward. The mechanism is a rolling buffer; the experience is simply having a little time in reserve.

Built, not mocked

What works now

The hardware experiment runs on a Raspberry Pi 5 with a Hauppauge dual OTA tuner and an indoor amplified antenna. The prototype is being built from the actual broadcast primitives rather than designing a finished media-center interface first.

  • Both Hauppauge tuners recognized natively by Linux.
  • Strong local OTA reception and channel scanning established.
  • Native transport-stream recording and fullscreen HDMI playback proven.
  • Live OTA television and programmatic channel switching proven.
  • 1080i MPEG-2 playback tested successfully on Raspberry Pi 5.
  • Lightweight fullscreen chrome prototyped independently from the video renderer.

Next: find the cleanest low-overhead rendering path for live video so buffering, controls, additional signal sources and creative tools have room to run.

Origin

An old question, newly buildable.

The first Shuffletube interface concept was designed in 2006 around a simple idea: television offered many simultaneous choices, but very little direct control over them. Twenty years later, inexpensive tuners and small computers make it possible to investigate that question as working hardware instead of a screen concept.
